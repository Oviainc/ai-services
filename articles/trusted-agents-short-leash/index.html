Trusted, Used Daily — and Kept on a Short Leash

The study

G2's “Stop Selling Autonomy” reads the review record instead of running another opinion poll: 3,222 approved enterprise reviews, from companies with more than 1,000 employees, across 231 AI agent products in five categories, published between January 2025 and October 23, 2026. Reviews of this kind carry the texture surveys miss — praise and complaints written after real rollouts, by the people who live with the results. The headline finding is a split: enterprises trust their agents and use them more every quarter, and almost none of them will let an agent act alone.

Trust without letting go

The numbers draw the line precisely. G2 reports that 77% of enterprise reviewers trust their agent quite a bit or very much. Asked how much autonomy that trust buys, the answers tighten: 60% choose autonomy with guardrails, 21% want limited autonomy, 11% want a human approving every action, and only 7% would grant total autonomy. Trust, in other words, answers whether the agent does the job well. Autonomy answers whether anyone checks the work before the work lands — on a customer, a record, or a payment. Buyers keep those two questions separate, and the people carrying the risk hold the line tightest: administrators and executive sponsors grant total autonomy least of all.

Visibility beats reliability

Asked what would raise trust further, 79% of reviewers picked a visibility or control fix — the agent explaining its decisions, more guardrail settings, a warning before a questionable action — while only 21% asked for better reliability. The scoring detail underneath agrees: output accuracy earns top marks far more easily than explanations do. Most buyers already believe the agent usually gets the answer right. What holds trust back is seeing how the answer was reached, and knowing what happens on the steps where the answer is wrong. Oversight has a price, and buyers pay it knowingly: G2's related survey work names the overhead a distrust tax — hours of supervision and rework spent every week because the evidence trail is thinner than the capability.

The adoption drag

Two quieter findings explain why the leash stays short in practice. Setup and complexity form the top complaint in the reviews, named in 25% of dislikes — raised even by reviewers who score their agent nine or ten out of ten. And depth of use is sliding: the average share of intended users who fully adopted their agent fell from 68% in 2024 to 55% in 2026. An agent that is hard to set up, hard to explain and used by barely half the team never earns the organisational confidence that looser autonomy would require. The leash, read this way, is less about fear of the technology and more about unfinished operational work around the technology.

What builders should sell instead

The practical conclusion writes the roadmap for anyone shipping agents. Lead with the leash: guardrails the buyer sets, decisions the agent can explain, warnings before risky steps, and costs that stay visible as they accumulate. Prove one narrow, measurable win first — a back-office task with a human approving the output — and let the leash lengthen as the evidence grows. Business intelligence, data and automation work at Ovia starts from exactly that surface: dashboards, audit trails and approval points are where trust is manufactured. Context engineering at Ovia since December 2022 treats those controls as part of the agent's context, not as packaging around the agent. Autonomy may still be the destination. Supervision is the product being bought on the way there.
