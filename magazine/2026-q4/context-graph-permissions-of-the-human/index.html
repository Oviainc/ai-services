Context Travels, Authority Stays Personal

ZoomInfo has placed its go-to-market context graph inside Snowflake, where agents read company and contact intelligence through the permissions of the person asking. The context travels everywhere; the authority stays personal.

Ovia Inc · Studio and Lab · 6 min read

Business Intelligence · Data · Governance

Key points

  • ZoomInfo MCP and API now run inside the Snowflake AI Data Cloud through GTM.AI.
  • Agents in CoWork and Cortex read a graph of 100M+ companies and 500M contacts, in ZoomInfo figures.
  • Every call runs on OAuth 2.0 and inherits the role-based permissions of the user asking.
  • An audit framework records who asked, through which agent, under whose authority.
  • Bulk enrichment runs 10,000 records per request and 200,000 per query.
“The agent reads with borrowed eyes — and the eyes belong to a named person.”

A context graph is only as useful as the questions allowed to reach it. Sales and marketing teams have wanted, for years, to ask their market the way analysts ask a warehouse: in plain language, across every company and contact on record, with the answer arriving inside the tools already open. The obstacle was never the asking. The obstacle was authority: a graph holding intelligence on more than a hundred million companies cannot simply open itself to any agent that wanders past. On October 30, 2026, ZoomInfo answered with a design worth studying well beyond sales.

01 — Inside the warehouse where the work already happens

ZoomInfo's MCP server and API now run natively inside the Snowflake AI Data Cloud, delivered through its GTM.AI platform. Agents working in Snowflake CoWork and Cortex, the places where data teams already build and ask, can reach ZoomInfo intelligence without exporting records into yet another silo. Placement is the story: the context graph moved to the data, instead of the data moving to the graph, and the warehouse's own agents gained a sense the estate did not have before, a live view of the market outside the building.

02 — The graph itself

What agents can reach is the GTM Context Graph: more than 100 million companies, 500 million contacts and billions of buying signals, in ZoomInfo's own figures. The interface is a working set rather than a single search box: Search, Enrich, Lookup, Bulk and Monitoring endpoints cover finding, completing, verifying, mass-processing and watching records. The bulk numbers set the scale plainly, with enrichment accepting 10,000 records per request and 200,000 per query. Numbers like those move the graph out of the lookup category and into the pipeline category, where enrichment becomes a stage in a data product rather than a favour asked of a browser tab.

03 — Permissions of the human

Access runs on OAuth 2.0, and the decisive detail is inheritance: every call an agent makes carries the role-based permissions of the user behind the session. An agent working for a regional sales lead sees the graph as that lead sees it, no wider. An agent working for a data steward with broader rights sees more, and the difference is a personnel fact, not a model behaviour. Around the calls sits an audit framework, so the organisation can reconstruct who asked, through which agent, for what, and under whose authority. Accountability survives delegation because the delegation itself is written down.

04 — Why the pattern travels

Business intelligence has quietly run on this pattern for years. A dashboard never held rights of its own; the dashboard inherited the reader's, and row-level security decided what a chart was allowed to show. Agents are the same discipline at machine speed, and the ZoomInfo design simply refuses to unlearn it. The alternative, agents holding standing credentials of their own, is how context graphs become breach graphs. As context layers arrive in engineering, finance, support and security, the governing question stays identical: does the agent inherit a person's authority, or hold authority of its own? Personal inheritance keeps accountability legible, and legible accountability is what lets a board say yes.

05 — Watch items, honestly noted

The October 31 industry roundup adds the caveats a careful buyer keeps. Pricing was not disclosed, and a ZoomInfo subscription is required, so the graph's cost sits inside a commercial conversation rather than on a public page. The endpoints are sales-oriented today, aimed at go-to-market work. Neither caveat dents the pattern. Patterns escape their first market: permission-inheriting context access, audited end to end, is how every serious context graph will be served within a few product cycles. The teams that internalise the pattern now, in business intelligence first, will recognise it everywhere it lands next.

06 — The checklist the design hands the buyer

The design hands buyers a test that fits inside one meeting. Sign in as a regional sales lead and have an agent in Cortex ask a market question; then sign in as a data steward and ask the same question again. If both sessions draw on the same visible graph, inheritance is a slide in a deck, not a mechanism. In the ZoomInfo design the difference is structural: OAuth 2.0 carries the asker’s role-based permissions on every call, so the graph an agent sees is the graph its person is entitled to see — a hundred million companies on paper, and only the entitled slice in practice.

The second test is reconstruction. Take any answer an agent produced last week and ask the audit framework to rebuild the moment: who asked, through which agent, under whose authority, against which records. The framework described in the release records exactly those facts. That is the standard worth holding every context vendor to, because delegation without a written trail is where accountability goes to die; with one, a board or a customer can follow a decision back to a named person, and the agent’s work stays defensible long after the session ends.

The third test is scale. Enrichment at 10,000 records per request and 200,000 per query means one instruction can touch a market-sized slice of the graph, which is precisely why permissions and audit cannot be features added later. We read the launch as a template: place the context where the work already happens, let agents inherit a person’s authority rather than hold authority of their own, and log the delegation itself. Vendors that meet those three terms will be easy to approve. Vendors that meet none of them are asking the organisation to lend its authority to software, and hope.
