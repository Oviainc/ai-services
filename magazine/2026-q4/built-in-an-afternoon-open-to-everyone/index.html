Built in an Afternoon, Open to Everyone

Building is now the easy part. In thousands of live applications built in an afternoon, the data inside could be read without logging in at all, the doors were the problem.

Inès Duarte · Ovia Inc · Studio and Lab · 7 min read

The applications were real, and many of them were good. A lifestyle forum with more than eighty thousand members. Event organisers selling tickets, restaurants selling food, a property management firm keeping its correspondence in order. Each one had been built the modern way: described in plain language to an AI app builder, assembled in hours, connected to a database, launched. Building, it turns out, is now the easy part. The doors were the problem. In thousands of these live applications, the data inside could be read without logging in at all.

That is the finding of a study by the Hamburg research firm Crissy Field, produced in collaboration with Handelsblatt and reported on November 2, 2026. The researchers examined 159,041 projects built on Lovable, the Stockholm platform founded in 2023 by Anton Osika and Fabian Hedin, which turns plain language descriptions into working web applications for people with little or no programming experience. The sample was drawn from the Common Crawl web archive, covering projects from December 2025 to September 2026. The question the study asked was narrow and fair: is sensitive data in these applications protected against access without a login?

Key points

  • 159,041 Lovable projects were examined from the Common Crawl archive, December 2025 to September 2026; 34,545 used Supabase, and 24,664 sent database records as soon as they loaded, before any login.
  • In 10,560 tables across 7,403 of those websites, further read only requests, made with the same public API key the sites ship to every browser, returned records containing email addresses.
  • The 30 percent figure is not a confirmed leak rate: the researchers say so themselves, the sample is not representative of all Lovable applications, and whether any exposed data was ever misused could not be established.
  • A misconfiguration that repeats is a template: the same service, the same connection method, the same missing or misconfigured row level security, an approach that opens one application can be tried against the next with little extra effort.

01 · What the researchers did, and what they found

Of the projects examined, 34,545 used Supabase, the database service the study focused on. Some 24,664 websites sent database records as soon as they loaded, before any login, which by itself proves nothing, since some data is meant to be public. So the researchers did what any visitor could do: they made further read only requests, using the same public API key the sites themselves ship to every browser, and asked the databases for more. In 10,560 tables across 7,403 of those websites, the databases answered with records containing email addresses.

The cases behind the count are what make the study hard to wave away. The lifestyle forum exposed more than 80,000 profiles, full names, email addresses, dates of birth, and more than 200,000 private chat messages. Event organisers and restaurants exposed tens of thousands of records from ticket and food sales, including phone numbers, postal addresses, payment information, and ticket codes. A property management company exposed AI generated summaries of internal emails about tenants, owners, and partners. One service provider exposed more than 1,000 records containing identity document details: passport numbers, expiration dates, nationalities. Elsewhere the researchers found tens of thousands of contracts and invoices, personal photo albums, internal WhatsApp communications, and, in a single case, 280,000 emails a provider had sent to its customers, all readable.

02 · The numbers, held to their proper size

The study is unusually careful about its own limits, and this piece keeps them at full size. The headline proportion, records with email addresses reachable in 30 percent of the closely examined sites, is not a confirmed leak rate, and the researchers say so themselves. Some of those addresses may be public contacts or test data; other personal records, containing no email address, would have been missed entirely; the two effects offset each other to an unknown degree. The Common Crawl sample is not representative of all Lovable applications. The researchers worked read only, downloaded parts of datasets rather than wholes, and the figures count only what they actually retrieved, the true contents may be larger. Whether any of the exposed data was ever misused, they could not establish. What survives every one of those caveats is the core observation, and it needs no percentage at all: across thousands of live applications, the same kind of door stood open in the same kind of wall.

03 · Why the doors repeat

Access control mistakes can happen in any application, written by hand or generated. What changes with an AI builder is the pattern. Database integrations on the platform follow recurring shapes, the same service, the same connection method, the same missing or misconfigured row level security that decides which records a visitor may see. A misconfiguration that repeats is no longer a mistake; it is a template. An approach that opens one application can be tried against the next with little extra effort, and the people least equipped to spot the flaw are the platform's intended users, who were told, accurately, about building, that no technical knowledge was needed. Lovable's terms place responsibility for applications and data handling on its customers. The gap between that sentence and the marketing is where the data sat.

The report's conclusion deserves quoting in spirit because it is so unglamorous: automated checks are no substitute for a professional security assessment, and access permissions should be reviewed before an application handles real customer data, then reviewed again after significant changes. Nothing in that sentence is new. Everything in this study is what happens when it is skipped at scale.

A misconfiguration that repeats is no longer a mistake; it is a template.

04 · Building is not operating

There is a familiar story that says AI is replacing the builders. This study tells the truer one. Generation has become cheap: a working application, with user accounts and a database, in an afternoon. What did not become cheap is everything that makes an application safe to operate, access control, review, testing against the question an attacker would ask, and accountability when the answer is wrong. Those are skilled tasks, and the supply of people who can do them did not multiply just because the supply of applications did.

That gap is not a reason to stop building. It is a map of where the work now lives. Every generated application that touches real data creates a standing need: someone to check the doors, and to keep checking them after every significant change, because applications built in an afternoon get rebuilt in an afternoon too. The forum members whose messages were readable did nothing wrong. The restaurant customers did nothing wrong. They trusted that a finished looking product was a finished product. The distance between those two things, finished looking, and finished, is exactly the distance this study measured, one open door at a time.

All figures are as reported by Crissy Field. The 30 percent figure is the share of closely examined sites returning records containing email addresses under read only requests; the researchers state it is not a confirmed leak rate and the sample is not representative of all Lovable applications.
