One Hour of Audio, One Context

NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe holds a full hour of recorded audio in a single context and returns speakers, timestamps and answers. Sound stops being an attachment and becomes context.

Ovia Inc · Studio and Lab · 6 min read

Most organisational memory is sound. Deals are talked through, incidents are narrated, customers explain themselves out loud, and the written record keeps only the summary somebody had time to type. Transcription was supposed to fix that, and mostly produced a new chore: audio chopped into fragments, speakers blurred together, timestamps approximate, the transcript exiled to a file nobody queries. A model released on Hugging Face on October 28, 2026 treats the problem as one of context rather than conversion.

The release matters for where it landed: as open weights, with the training-data pipeline published beside the model. Openness is not a garnish here. An offline speech model that organisations can run against their own archives, inside their own walls, changes which recordings can be processed at all, including the sensitive ones that never leave the building.

01 — One hour, one context

NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Transcribe-30B-A4B accepts up to a full hour of recorded audio in a single pass, inside a 65,536-token context. The size of the name carries the architecture: roughly 30 billion parameters in total, about 4 billion active per token in a mixture-of-experts design, which is how an hour of audio fits in one context without an hour of compute following it. The model is deliberately offline, built for recorded audio rather than live streams, and that choice is a feature: the archive, not the microphone, is where unmined context sits.

One context is the operative phrase. Fragmented pipelines lose the thread of a conversation at every seam: a speaker introduced in minute three is a stranger again in minute forty, and a decision in minute fifty floats free of the objection raised in minute ten. A single pass over the whole hour keeps the thread intact, and the thread is where meaning lives.

02 — What comes back

The return is structured. Transcription covers 40 languages; translation covers 31 languages to and from English; speaker attribution covers 13 languages and up to eight speakers on one recording; word-level timestamps pin every word to its moment; and audio question answering, trained in English, lets an analyst ask the recording questions directly. Structure is the difference between a transcript and context. Speakers, timing and answers are what downstream systems, and agents, actually consume. Word-level timestamps deserve a sentence of their own: a claim in a transcript is a lead, while a claim with a timestamp is evidence, one step from the moment itself, and evidence is what reviews, disputes and audits run on.

“An hour of voices goes in as sound and comes back as structure: speakers, timestamps, answers.”

03 — The scoreboard

On the Hugging Face Open ASR English Leaderboard, the model posts a 5.33% average word error rate, first among open-weight models, per NVIDIA. The licence is OpenMDW-1.1, an open release in substance as well as in name. Leaderboards are not deployments, and English benchmarks are not the world's languages; even read carefully, though, the number marks the level at which an open model stops being the compromise option for recorded speech.

04 — The data pipeline is part of the release

Under the model sits Granary v2, the data pipeline NVIDIA used to prepare its training audio, published with NeMo recipes so other teams can reproduce the approach. The recipe counts 463,062 hours of audio across 48 languages. Publishing the pipeline beside the weights matters more than the weights alone: data preparation is where speech systems are actually won, and the recipe turns one lab's pipeline into shared infrastructure. For teams building speech systems, the recipes compress months of pipeline trial and error into a documented starting point, and the hour count signals the scale modern speech training quietly requires.

05 — Sound becomes first-class context

The audio roots behind Ovia, training in Audio Engineering and Sound Design at TREBAS, plus Hugging Face and NVIDIA certifications, make the operational reading direct. An hour of meeting, call or field recording that returns speakers, timestamps and answers is no longer an attachment to the record; the recording is the record, queryable like any other context an agent holds. The work that follows is integration: routing the recordings an organisation already keeps into pipelines that return structure, then wiring that structure into the same context layers the text record already feeds. Context engineering has spent years perfecting text. Sound was the largest room still outside the design. That room now has a door, and the door is open weights.

06 — What changes in the room where recordings pile up

The practical change lands in rooms that already record everything and use almost none of it. A support organisation holding a year of calls can run the model offline, inside its own walls, and ask the archive questions directly — audio question answering, trained in English, returns answers drawn from the recording itself — instead of commissioning a transcript review nobody staffs. Speaker attribution for up to eight voices and word-level timestamps mean the answer arrives with its evidence attached: who said it, and the second they said it.

The limits deserve the same plain reading as the numbers. Speaker attribution covers 13 languages against 40 for transcription, so a multilingual archive receives text in more languages than it receives named speakers. The 5.33% average word error rate is an English leaderboard average, per NVIDIA, not a promise made to a noisy field recording. And one hour is a boundary as well as a headline: a day-long workshop still arrives in pieces, even if each piece now holds together on its own. None of that shrinks the shift; it marks where engineering judgement still earns its keep.

Our reading, from the studio side: treat the recording as a primary source in the context layer, not as an attachment to a summary. Route the hour through the model once, keep speakers and timestamps as first-class fields beside the transcript, and let agents cite the moment instead of paraphrasing the meeting. That is the difference between an archive that answers and an archive that merely stores — and it is available as open weights, under the OpenMDW-1.1 licence, to any team willing to do the integration work.

Key points

  • Up to one hour of recorded audio in a single 65,536-token context.
  • Mixture-of-experts design: about 30B parameters in total, about 4B active.
  • 40 languages transcribed, 31 translated, 13 with speaker attribution, up to 8 speakers.
  • 5.33% average word error rate on the Open ASR English Leaderboard, first among open-weight models per NVIDIA.
  • Granary v2 pipeline and NeMo recipes published: 463,062 hours across 48 languages.
