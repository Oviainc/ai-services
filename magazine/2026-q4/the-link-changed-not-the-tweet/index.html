The Link Changed, Not the Tweet

A 2011 tweet now opens a page it never pointed at. Nothing was hacked and nothing was posted: the shortened link underneath it was issued a second time, to someone else.

Inès Duarte · Ovia Inc · Studio and Lab · 6 min read

On the morning of November 15, 2026, Burger King's account appeared to do something unforgivable. A tweet from July 24, 2011, advertising a restaurant locator, now opened Wikipedia's list of Nazi concentration camps. A post quoting it passed two million views within hours. Eighteen minutes before that link changed, a link in a McDonald's tweet from June 7, 2011 had been pointed at Wikipedia's article on the September 11 attacks.

Neither account was hacked. Nothing was posted, nothing was breached, no password was stolen. The tweets read today exactly as they read in 2011. What changed is where their links go, and the links were never the companies' to keep.

Key points

  • A shortened link's back-half is the whole asset: whoever holds it decides where every copy of that link leads, in every tweet, email, PDF and printed QR code that ever carried it.
  • Bitly's public information pages give the Burger King link a creation time of 05:32 UTC on November 15, 2026, and the McDonald's link 05:14 the same morning: a 2011 link carrying a 2026 creation date was issued a second time, to someone else.
  • Of 97 Bitly links extracted from an archive of Donald Trump's tweets, 40 are still the original links and 57 are not; 49 of the 57 were re-created in a seventeen-minute run on May 26, 2023, yet still lead where the tweets suggest.
  • The remedy is an audit, not a product: read each old link's creation date and destination, compare both against the record of what was published, and list the dead ones: a dead short link is an unguarded door with the company's name still on it.

01 · The ending of the link

A shortened link has two parts: the shortener's domain, and the ending after the slash, called the back-half. The back-half is the whole asset. Whoever holds it decides where every copy of that link leads, in every tweet, email, PDF, annual report and printed QR code that ever carried it. The publisher does not hold it. The shortener issues it, and the shortener's records are the only registry of who holds what.

Bitly's own help pages state that a back-half cannot be reused, even after the account behind it is deleted. The creation dates collected on November 15 do not fit that statement. Bitly keeps a public information page for every link, reached by adding a plus sign to the address, showing the destination and the moment of creation. The Burger King link's page gives a creation time of 05:32 UTC on November 15, 2026. A link printed in a tweet from 2011 can only carry a 2026 creation date if the same ending was issued a second time, to someone else. The McDonald's link was re-created at 05:14 the same morning. The mechanism is not exotic: when an old link stops existing, its ending can be claimed, and the new holder inherits every printed and posted copy of it, silently, with no notification to the brand whose name stands above it.

This has happened before, in miniature. In February 2025, Disruptionist reported that memecoin promoters had taken over Bitly links in 2013 posts and pointed them at token listings; a bug that let back-halves be claimed after deletion was reported fixed. The links created on November 15, 2026 show the same thing is possible now.

02 · Fifty-seven of ninety-seven

Ransomnews tested the scale on the most archived account on the platform. From a public archive of Donald Trump's tweets, it extracted 97 Bitly links first used between 2009 and 2013, and read each one's information page. Forty are still the original links. Fifty-seven are not.

The fifty-seven divide in a way that should worry a careful reader more than the headline cases do. Seven lead somewhere their tweets never pointed: four registered on November 15 itself, opening a Roblox profile, two YouTube videos and a Wikipedia article; others, registered in 2024 and 2025, opening domains that copy the names of crypto services, one a single letter away from a real analytics site, at a path named “eligibility/check”. One April 2011 tweet carried three links to videos of the account holder's children; all three have been replaced. And forty-nine of the fifty-seven were re-created years ago yet still lead where the tweets suggest, forty-eight of them inside a seventeen-minute run on May 26, 2023, roughly one every twenty seconds. Nobody knows who did that. It reads as a repair job by someone who knew where each link used to go, and it proves the endings had been free to claim since at least 2023.

A replaced link can be replaced again. When the domain behind one of the crypto lookalikes lapsed and was re-registered in November 2026, its new owner inherited whatever traffic a fifteen-year-old tweet still sends. The chain of custody of a public statement now runs: the brand wrote it, a shortener issued its address, a stranger re-registered the address, a second stranger bought the destination. At no point did the text change.

Bitly confirmed the issue on the evening of November 15, saying it had identified a problem that “allowed bad actors to repurpose certain old, inactive links”, suspended the accounts involved and began disabling the affected links; the Burger King and McDonald's links were deactivated within the day. CNN reported the same evening that old posts from Bill Gates, Justin Trudeau, Mike Pence, the FBI and the US Department of Defense were affected as well; that wider list is CNN's reporting, not independently checked in the source reporting this piece draws on, and it is printed here as such.

The tweet is the part of the record everyone watches. The link is the part that moves.

03 · The audit nobody runs

The uncomfortable part is not the vulnerability. It is the assumption underneath it, held by essentially every organisation with a decade of public posts: that a link, once published, is a fixed reference, like a page number in a printed book. It is not. It is a tenancy. The printed book can be re-pointed by whoever next rents the address, and the tenancy register is public, one plus sign away, and almost nobody reads it.

The remedy is correspondingly plain, and it is an audit, not a product. Every organisation holds a long tail of shortened links scattered across years of posts, documents, packaging and signage. Each one can be checked the way the reporters checked these: read the creation date, read the destination, compare both against the record of what was published. Links that no longer resolve to their purpose get replaced in the living documents, and the dead ones get listed, because a dead short link is not a harmless fossil. It is an unguarded door with the company's name still on it, waiting for the next person to claim the ending.

Creation dates, counts and quotations are as reported by Ransomnews on November 15, 2026. The wider list of affected accounts is CNN's reporting, carried in that source and printed here as such.
