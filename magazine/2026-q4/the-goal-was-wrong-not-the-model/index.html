The Goal Was Wrong, Not the Model

Replace the model with perfect physics and success barely moved. Rewrite the goal, chain the plan through subgoals, and the same model, with no retraining, went from 22 to 85.

Mara Kessler · Ovia Inc · Studio and Lab · 7 min read

The experiment sounds like a formality. Take a world model that plans by predicting the future, and replace its learned predictions with the truth: the perfect physics of the simulator itself. If the model is the bottleneck, success should soar. A researcher named Haddy Alchaer ran that swap on LeWM, a JEPA world model from the research line Yann LeCun has championed for years, across some of six hundred experiments on the model. Success moved from 78 percent to 77. The model, on the paths it knew, was already predicting about as well as reality.

Everything that followed in that long experimental series points the same way. Again and again, the large gains were sitting somewhere other than the model: in how the plan was chained, and above all in how the goal had been written down.

Key points

  • Used exactly as published, with the goal 75 steps away, LeWM succeeds 22 percent of the time; brought to 25 steps, just under 90 percent, a horizon of about 25 steps of trustworthy foresight.
  • Perfect physics for the whole 75 steps lifted success to 42 percent. The untouched model, given a fresh subgoal every 25 steps, reached 80; chaining through a graph of its own demonstrations reached 85.
  • Scored on the block alone instead of the block and the pusher together, the perfect-physics planner went from nine successes to thirty-nine on the same fifty harder tasks.
  • A goal is a set of acceptable end states, not a picture of one of them.

01 · The task, and the 25 step horizon

LeWM works in three parts. An encoder turns each frame of the world into a compact learned description. A predictor forecasts the next description given an action. A planner searches hundreds of action sequences inside the predictor and keeps the one whose predicted end lands closest to the goal. The test bench is Push-T, a simulation in which a robot pusher nudges a grey T shaped block across a table until the block sits on the green target shown in a goal image.

Used exactly as published, with the goal 75 steps away, LeWM succeeds 22 percent of the time. Brought to 25 steps, it succeeds just under 90 percent of the time, within a point of the perfect physics version. The model has a horizon: about 25 steps of trustworthy foresight, then fog.

The standard response to a horizon is a better model. The experiments priced that response precisely. Handing the planner perfect physics for the whole 75 steps lifted success to 42 percent, twenty points for replacing the model with the truth. Leaving the model untouched and simply giving it a fresh subgoal every 25 steps lifted success to 80. Chaining the plan through a graph built from the model's own memory of past demonstrations, with a cost charged on every jump between demonstrations, reached 85, the same score as a planner handed ideal subgoals by an expert. No retraining. No new weights. The capability was already in the building; the plan had been asking for it in the wrong shape.

02 · The pusher in the picture

Then the goal itself was caught misbehaving. A goal in Push-T is an image, and an image of the scene has to show the pusher somewhere. The frames used as goals came from expert demonstrations, so each one silently recorded where the expert pusher happened to be standing at that moment, a fact about the demonstration, not a requirement of the task.

A planner scored against the whole image cannot tell the difference. It tries to reproduce everything in the picture, pusher included. Alchaer isolated the effect with the model removed entirely: the perfect physics planner, run twice over the same fifty harder tasks, once scored on the block and the pusher together, once on the block alone. Nine successes became thirty-nine. Thirty tasks flipped from failure to success when the pusher was left out of the cost; not one flipped the other way. On the flipped tasks, the full image planner had done something almost poignant: it placed the pusher within half a pixel of where the goal image wanted it, and lost the block on the way. It solved the goal it was given. The goal it was given was wrong.

LeWM cannot simply be told to ignore the pusher, since its goal lives inside a learned embedding where block and pusher are fused. The workaround is elegant in its plainness: score each plan against sixteen versions of the goal, the block fixed, the pusher moved to positions drawn from real demonstrations, and keep the closest match. On the harder test that doubled success, from 11 to 23 percent, again without retraining. Sixteen acceptable pictures in place of one exact one.

A goal is a set of acceptable end states, not a picture of one of them.

03 · Read the caveat before quoting the numbers

The Magazine prints its limits where readers can see them. This is one researcher's experimental series, published as a long research post rather than a peer reviewed paper, on one simulated benchmark. Alchaer is candid that the standard Push-T test flatters its subjects: goals are drawn along demonstration paths, the usual sample is small, and a change of random seed alone flipped 38 of 250 episodes between success and failure. On the harder test the researcher built, the best planner holds the block on target 56 percent of the time. The story is not that the problem is solved. The story is where the unsolved part lives.

04 · What a buyer of agents should take home

Translate the laboratory into the office and the mapping is direct. A business agent handed a goal as a single exact artefact, match this output, reproduce this document, end in precisely this state, is a planner scored against the whole picture, pusher included. It will spend its effort reproducing incidental details of whoever demonstrated the task, and it will be marked a failure for losing the block: the outcome that actually mattered, sitting unspecified inside an over specified goal.

The remedies translate too. Chain the long task through subgoals at the horizon the system demonstrably handles, instead of demanding one heroic plan. Score the result against a set of acceptable end states, the range of finishes a competent person would sign, rather than against one golden example. And when a deployment disappoints, audit the goal before shopping for a larger model. In six hundred experiments, replacing the model with perfection bought twenty points, and rewriting how success was defined bought sixty.

None of this is an argument against better models. It is an argument about sequence. Specification is cheaper than capability, and it is purchased first or paid for later, in failures misdiagnosed as the model's fault. The studios and teams that get durable results from agents are, increasingly, the ones that treat the definition of done as the product: written down, tested, argued over. The model was rarely the bottleneck. The goal was wrong, and goals, unlike models, can be fixed by Friday.

All experiment figures are as reported by the researcher in the source article; the study is a single experimental series on a simulated benchmark and has not been peer reviewed. The piece reports it as such.
